\section{Dynamic Responses to a Monetary Policy Shock in the Euro Area}\label{sec:results}
In this section, we discuss the responses to a restrictive monetary policy shock in the euro area. These responses are computed while explicitly conditioning on the US monetary policy stance. This exercise allows us to answer the question of how the monetary policy effect might change in the face of a divergent Fed policy. We therefore report and analyze impulse response functions (IRFs) based on our framework outlined in detail in \autoref{sec:framework}. 

{\color{red}
The central object of interest is how the transmission of an ECB surprise varies with the \textit{entire} range of the Fed's stance, not merely at two extremes. We therefore lead with the continuum of responses: because the transition function $g(z^\ast_t)$ delivers a distinct set of dynamic coefficients for every value of the Fed's stance, our model traces out a continuous mapping from the stance to the response of each variable. \autoref{fig:headline} presents this mapping for the one-week-ahead reaction of nominal government bond yields (top row), inflation swaps (middle row), and real yields (bottom row) at four selected maturities over the full 2005--2025 sample, plotting the response against the percentile of the Fed's stance. Reading the figure row-wise delivers the paper's central result together with its economic decomposition; note that, draw by draw, the nominal response equals the real response plus the response of inflation compensation.

The headline finding is that the Fed's stance does not shift the pass-through of ECB surprises up or down --- it \textit{rotates} it. When the Fed is accommodative (fifth percentile of the stance), a restrictive ECB surprise raises nominal yields by $1.3$ basis points at one year and by close to $1.5$ basis points at maturities from three to thirty years. When the Fed is restrictive (ninety-fifth percentile), the same surprise raises the one-year yield by $2.0$ basis points --- more, not less, than under an accommodative Fed --- but the reaction then falls monotonically with maturity, to $1.2$ basis points at five years, $0.2$ at ten years, and \textit{below} zero at the long end. The nominal reaction remains significantly positive across the entire range of the Fed's stance at maturities up to five years, whereas at ten, fifteen, and thirty years it ceases to be distinguishable from zero once the stance passes roughly its sixty-fifth, sixtieth, and fifty-fifth percentile, respectively. The state dependence of ECB transmission is thus a phenomenon of the \textit{long} end of the curve.

This maturity pattern is what the mechanisms set out in the introduction predict, and it is informative precisely because it is not uniform. Short-dated yields are governed by the expected path of the ECB's own policy rate, which responds to a domestic surprise in much the same way whatever the external environment; under the expectations hypothesis there is little room for the Fed's stance to matter at this segment, and empirically it does not. Long-dated yields, by contrast, embed a substantial term premium, and it is this component that is compressed --- and rendered more sensitive to policy news --- when US policy is accommodative and global risk appetite is high \citep{miranda2020us}. When the Fed itself is tight, that amplification is absent and an ECB tightening no longer moves the long end at all.

The middle and bottom rows show that the fading of the \textit{nominal} response at long maturities does not mean that policy stops working; at the short end, in particular, it conceals a pronounced change in composition. Under an accommodative Fed, inflation swap rates barely move beyond the very short end (below $0.3$ basis points from three years out), so the nominal reaction is almost entirely a real-rate reaction. Under a restrictive Fed, inflation compensation falls significantly at every maturity, and strongly so at the short end ($-1.9$ basis points at one year, $-1.0$ at three years). The mirror image appears in the bottom row: the one-year real yield rises by $3.9$ basis points under a restrictive Fed, against $0.6$ under an accommodative one. An ECB tightening delivered while the Fed is hawkish therefore tightens real short-term financing conditions \textit{more} forcefully than the flat nominal response suggests, and it does so by lowering expected inflation rather than by raising the nominal rate. At long maturities the real response fades along with the nominal one, leaving the term-premium interpretation intact. A complementary heatmap over the \textit{full} term structure and all stance percentiles is provided in \autoref{fig:continuumheat} in Appendix~\ref{app:A}.

The inclusion of the pronounced Fed--ECB divergence of 2022--2024 in the estimation sample is what makes this continuum so sharply identified: it supplies exactly the restrictive-Fed observations that were scarce before the pandemic. This continuum, rather than a comparison of two hand-picked extremes, is the empirical heart of the paper.

We then unpack this mapping over the impulse horizon. To do so it is convenient to fix two reference points along the continuum. The first sets the threshold variable $z_t^\ast$ to its $95\%$ percentile, a strongly \textit{tight} Fed; the second sets it to the $5\%$ percentile, a strongly \textit{loose} Fed. Comparing these two extremes isolates the largest possible difference in transmission but, precisely because it discards the intermediate stances where most of our observations lie, it also yields the noisiest contrasts; we therefore treat the two-extreme comparison as illustrative and rely on the full continuum in \autoref{fig:headline} for inference. In the figures that follow, the top panel shows the responses under the two reference stances over the impulse horizon and the bottom panel their difference; the corresponding impact responses across the continuum of stances are those already reported in \autoref{fig:headline}.

The remainder of this section proceeds as follows. We first discuss the dynamic responses of government bond yields; the corresponding dynamic responses of inflation swaps and real yields, whose impact behavior is already summarized by \autoref{fig:headline}, are delegated to Appendix~\ref{app:A}, as are the responses of the remaining macro-financial variables --- the exchange rate, stock prices, and systemic stress. \autoref{sec:extended} discusses what the 2022--2024 divergence episode contributes to identification. All results are estimated on the full 2005--2025 sample --- the extended inflation swap data now cover the entire sample period --- and are based on the updated data vintage described in \autoref{sec:Data}.

In both regimes, government bond yields rise on impact after a restrictive monetary policy shock, and in both the reaction is concentrated in the first week and has died out within roughly six weeks --- the pattern one expects of efficient financial markets. What differs across regimes is not the timing but the maturity profile. Comparing the blue and red lines in panel (a), the two regimes are statistically indistinguishable at the short end: the one-year yield rises by $1.3$ basis points under an accommodative Fed and $2.0$ under a restrictive one, and the three-year responses ($1.5$ versus $1.9$) likewise differ insignificantly. The gap then opens up, and changes sign, as maturity increases. At five years the difference is $-0.3$ basis points, at ten years $-1.3$, and it widens monotonically to $-2.1$ basis points at thirty years, where the response under a restrictive Fed is negative. Panel (b) shows this difference to be significantly negative at the $68\%$ level for every maturity from seven years upwards, and insignificant at one, three, and five years --- a clean monotone pattern that matches the continuum in \autoref{fig:headline} and, as discussed there, the term-premium mechanism that motivates it.

The difference is estimated most precisely in the impact week; over the remainder of the horizon it is largely indistinguishable from zero, with a brief echo around the fourth week that is visible in both regimes and at all maturities. We attach little weight to that echo: at weekly frequency and with the shrinkage prior in place, it is small relative to the impact effect and does not accumulate into a level effect (see \autoref{fig:IRFcum} in Appendix~\ref{app:A}). More generally, we read the two-extreme comparison as illustrative: it contrasts two tail stances and therefore discards the intermediate observations that carry most of the identifying information, which is why the credible sets in panel (b) are wide beyond the impact week. The continuum in \autoref{fig:headline} is the sharper instrument, and it delivers the same conclusion with far greater precision.}

\rev{One feature of the accommodative-Fed responses deserves comment: they are nearly flat across the maturity spectrum, the thirty-year yield reacting about as strongly as the one-year yield. This is plausibly a feature of how our surprise is constructed rather than a puzzle. The measure is the sum of the target, forward guidance, and quantitative easing factors of \cite{altavilla2019measuring}, and the latter two load precisely on the middle and long end of the curve; a composite that mixes them should be expected to move long maturities roughly as much as short ones. Under a restrictive Fed this flat profile gives way to a steeply declining one, which is the state dependence documented above.}

\rev{The apparently puzzling feature of these responses --- inflation swap rates that \textit{rise} after a monetary tightening, most visibly at short maturities and under an accommodative Fed --- deserves a careful discussion rather than a passing remark. Three considerations rationalize it. First, and most importantly, inflation-linked swap rates are not pure expectation measures: they are the sum of expected inflation, an inflation risk premium, and a liquidity premium. \cite{burban2021ILSrisk} show that the premium components account for a substantial share of the variation of euro area ILS rates at exactly the horizons where our positive responses appear, and that the premium can respond positively to shocks that raise inflation uncertainty. A policy surprise that raises uncertainty about the future path of policy can therefore lift the swap \textit{rate} even while lowering expected inflation. Consistent with this interpretation, the positive reaction is concentrated at the short end --- where the premium components are most volatile --- and is absent from the medium and long segments of the curve, where the responses carry the theoretically expected negative sign. Second, although our surprise measure is cleansed of central bank information effects through the poor man's sign restriction of \cite{jarocinski2020deconstructing}, this restriction disciplines the stock market reaction only; residual information effects that operate through inflation compensation cannot be fully excluded, and would push short-dated swap rates in precisely this direction. Indeed, when we re-estimate the model with the fully information-purged surprises of \cite{jarocinski2020deconstructing} (\autoref{sec:results_rob}), the positive short-end response is attenuated. Third, the effect is quantitatively small (below one basis point at the posterior median) and only marginally significant, so we caution against over-interpreting it. What is robust is the \textit{difference} in swap responses across Fed regimes at medium maturities, which is what our research question requires.}

\subsection{Assessment of the real rate effects}\label{sec:realrate}
\rev{The real-rate analysis in this subsection, together with the inflation-swap responses above, is based on the full system estimated over 2005--2022, the sample for which the inflation-swap curve is available (\autoref{sec:Data}); the corresponding figures therefore end in 2022 rather than 2026.} Our model also allows us to examine how real interest rates evolve after a restrictive EA monetary policy shock and how these reactions interact with a given US monetary policy stance. The corresponding real interest rate responses are shown in \autoref{fig:IRFreal} (a) - (c). Again, starting from a restrictive US stance, we see that when the euro area experiences a contractionary monetary policy shock, real interest rates rise for all maturities considered over the entire impulse horizon. However, the effect is most pronounced in the short and medium term and less pronounced for real rates at ten years and longer, eventually becoming insignificant in the impact. In contrast, when the shock occurs during an expansionary Fed stance, we observe heterogeneous dynamics across the term structure. While shorter maturities up to five years start to decline before mean reversion sets in after one to two weeks, medium and longer maturities show an increase. 

This suggests that if monetary policy is judged by its effect on real interest rates, a policy surprise during a restrictive U.S. stance leads to positive real interest rate reactions in the first four weeks after the shock. Under an expansionary Fed stance, the immediate increase in real rates turns into a sharp negative reaction after about two weeks for short-term maturities. The differences between the IRFs do not include zero and are thus statistically significant, except for some medium-term maturities, as shown in panel (b). According to the construction of real interest rates, their strong reactions are a consequence of the more pronounced reactions of government bonds under an expansionary US stance. Interestingly, the different reaction patterns seem to be less pronounced in the middle segment of the real yield curve.

\subsection{Controlling for global factors}\label{sec:global}
A natural concern with our identification strategy is that the Fed's stance may partly reflect common global conditions rather than US monetary policy per se. During a synchronized global inflation cycle, for instance, the Fed may be tight at the same time as commodity prices, the dollar, and global risk aversion move, and it could be these global forces, rather than US policy, that alter the transmission of ECB surprises. We address this concern in two complementary ways.

First, we augment the vector $\bm y_t$ with three global variables that are available at daily frequency and span the main dimensions of the global cycle: the Brent oil price (in percentage changes), the broad nominal US dollar index (in percentage changes), and the VIX as a proxy for global risk aversion. These variables enter both regimes and are allowed to react to, and feed back into, all EA quantities. \autoref{fig:IRFglobal} shows the yield curve responses from this extended model. They are remarkably close to the baseline: yield reactions to a restrictive ECB surprise remain significantly stronger under an accommodative Fed, with the one-week-ahead difference at short maturities significant at the $68\%$ level, and the maturity profile of the differences is unchanged. The global variables themselves react little to the ECB surprise, consistent with the euro area being small relative to global financial markets.

Second, we purge the stance measure itself of the global cycle. We regress $z_t^{\ast}$ on year-on-year oil price growth, year-on-year growth of the broad dollar index, and the level of the VIX, and use the residual --- the component of the Fed's stance that is orthogonal to these global factors --- as the transition variable. The resulting regime allocation is nearly identical to the baseline (the correlation of the posterior mean of $S_t$ across the two specifications is $0.95$), confirming that our stance measure is not simply a repackaged global cycle. The impulse responses retain the same pattern, although the differences between regimes lose some statistical precision, which is expected given that the purging step removes variation that is informative about the stance itself. Taken together, these exercises indicate that the state dependence we document is attributable to the US policy stance and not to omitted global variables, although we acknowledge that no reduced-form exercise can fully isolate US monetary policy from the global conditions to which it responds.

\subsection{The Fed's stance, the euro area cycle, and asymmetries}\label{sec:confound}
A second concern is that the Fed's stance may act as a stand-in for the state of the \textit{euro area} economy: the periods in which the Fed was accommodative overlap with the great recession, the sovereign debt crisis, and the effective lower bound episode, so stronger transmission under a loose Fed might simply reflect the well-studied state dependence of monetary policy over the business cycle \citep{tenreyro2016pushing}. Three pieces of evidence argue against this interpretation.

First, we re-estimate the model replacing the Fed's stance with a euro area business cycle indicator (the German weekly activity index, smoothed over one quarter) as the transition variable. If the Fed's stance were merely a cyclical proxy, the two specifications should sort the sample into similar regimes. They do not: the correlation between the posterior means of $S_t$ across the two models is $0.11$. The Fed-stance regimes split the pre-2008 boom from the post-crisis decade, whereas the cycle regimes track the 2008--2009 and 2011--2013 recessions and the subsequent recovery; the two classifications disagree over most of the sample. State dependence with respect to the domestic cycle and with respect to the foreign policy stance are thus empirically distinct objects in our data.

Second, the pattern survives when we allow for asymmetries between tightening and easing surprises --- a further possibility worth ruling out, since a model that pools both may attribute to the foreign stance what is in fact a sign asymmetry. Estimating the model separately with only restrictive and only expansionary surprises delivers the same qualitative result in both cases: responses are stronger when the Fed is accommodative. The credible sets widen, as each specification discards roughly half of the identifying events, and the one-week-ahead differences are no longer significant at the $68\%$ level maturity by maturity; but the sign and shape of the state dependence are preserved, indicating that it is not an artifact of pooling.

Third, the 2022--2024 episode discussed in \autoref{sec:extended} provides the cleanest separation: it combines a decisively tight Fed with a euro area economy that was neither at the lower bound nor in a financial crisis, and the state dependence remains.

\subsection{Persistence and level effects}\label{sec:persistence}
The impulse responses shown so far may convey the impression that monetary policy effects vanish within a month, which would sit uneasily with the macro literature. This impression rests on a units confusion that we now make explicit: the model is specified in \textit{first differences} of the term structure factors (see \autoref{sec:Data}), so the plotted objects are responses of weekly \textit{changes} in yields. A response of yield changes that returns to zero after four weeks does not mean that the effect on yields disappears --- it means that yields settle at a new level. \autoref{fig:IRFcum} accumulates the responses and displays the implied level effects: after a restrictive ECB surprise under an accommodative Fed, the 10-year yield remains above its pre-shock level over the entire response horizon, whereas under a tight Fed the cumulated effect turns negative --- the level counterpart of the reversal discussed above. The regime differences are thus persistent in levels.

Two further checks confirm that the speed of mean reversion in the differenced responses is not an artifact of a too-short lag length or of the shrinkage prior. Extending the lag length from four to eight weeks leaves the responses essentially unchanged, as does adding a weekly real activity indicator to the system (thereby allowing for feedback from the real economy at a weekly frequency). We report these results in \autoref{app:rob}. We agree with the broader point that weekly financial data are not the natural laboratory for tracing out effects on slow-moving macro aggregates at business cycle horizons; a mixed-frequency extension that couples our weekly financial block with monthly macro variables is an interesting avenue that we leave for future work, and we now flag it explicitly in the conclusions.

\subsection{The 2022--2024 divergence episode as an identification check}\label{sec:extended}
An obvious objection to our interpretation is that the Fed's stance may merely stand in for the state of the world: over most of the sample, the periods in which the Fed was accommodative coincide with the post-2008 recessions, the sovereign debt crisis, and the effective-lower-bound decade in the euro area. Stronger transmission under a loose Fed could then simply reflect the well-documented state dependence of monetary policy over the domestic business cycle, or the peculiarities of the lower-bound environment, rather than the US policy stance itself. For the pre-pandemic sample this confounding is hard to break, because the two configurations rarely decouple. The 2022--2024 episode breaks it. The Fed tightened aggressively while the euro area was neither in recession at the outset nor constrained by the lower bound, and no financial crisis was underway --- for the first time in our sample, a decisively restrictive Fed coincides with an otherwise unremarkable euro area economy. If the cycle interpretation were correct, transmission during this episode should look like transmission in other tranquil periods, i.e., strong; if anything, the reinforcing global tightening should make domestic policy \textit{more} powerful. The model finds the opposite. The regime allocation (\autoref{fig:stallo}) identifies the episode sharply --- the stance crosses neutral in mid-2022 and $S_t$ remains pinned at unity from 2023 through the end of the sample --- and the headline continuum (\autoref{fig:headline}) shows that the reaction of euro area yields to a restrictive ECB surprise remains \textit{larger} under an accommodative than under a restrictive Fed even with this episode included. Because the episode supplies precisely the restrictive-Fed observations that were scarce before the pandemic, it is not merely consistent with our mechanism but is what identifies the upper half of the continuum in the first place. We conclude that the state dependence we document is attributable to the Fed's stance rather than to the domestic cycle, and we interpret it as before: the compression of term premia and the search-for-yield environment associated with loose US policy amplify the marginal impact of domestic policy news, whereas in a tight global environment much of the adjustment is already priced in.

\subsection{Robustness}\label{sec:results_rob}
The rotation documented above rests on two constructed inputs and two calibrated parameters. The stance measure decides \textit{when} the Fed is restrictive, manufacturing the states; the surprise measure decides \textit{what} an ECB policy shock is, manufacturing the impulse; and the transition parameters $\mu$ and $\rho$ fix the location and sharpness of the boundary between regimes, neither of which the data identify (\autoref{app:prior}). An artifact would have to originate in one of these four. We vary each in turn, holding the others at baseline. Appendix~\ref{app:rob} collects the results; \autoref{tab:robspecs} lists the specifications, which reproduce from the replication code by name.

Replacing the stance with the shadow short rate of \cite{krippner2016ems} relative to $r^{\ast}$ leaves the regime dating essentially untouched: the two posterior transition paths correlate at $0.95$, and both assign $41.0\%$ of weeks to the restrictive regime. Replacing the identification with the median-rotation shocks of \cite{jarocinski2020deconstructing} changes the impulse itself and is the more demanding test. Moving the neutral cut by a quarter and by half a percentage point of the stance reclassifies up to $153$ of $1{,}095$ weeks and shifts the restrictive share from $34.8\%$ to $54.6\%$, while leaving the 2022--2024 episode restrictive throughout. Centering the steepness prior at seven rather than four leaves the responses virtually unchanged (correlation of posterior medians $0.997$, maximum deviation $0.24$ basis points).

Across all eight specifications the rotation is present without exception. The response at one and three years is significantly positive at every point of the stance distribution, whereas at ten years and beyond it ceases to be so between the fiftieth and seventieth percentile; in three specifications the thirty-year response turns significantly \textit{negative} once the Fed is sufficiently restrictive. The sign and the maturity profile of the state dependence are therefore not artifacts of measurement or calibration: the posterior probability of a positive loose-minus-tight gap at thirty years is at least $0.92$ in seven of the eight specifications and $0.82$ in the remaining one.

Its magnitude is another matter, and we do not claim robustness for it. The thirty-year gap ranges from $0.84$ basis points under the alternative identification to $2.90$ under the widest regime definition. Lowering the neutral cut confines the accommodative state to more deeply accommodative weeks and widens the gap monotonically, so this dependence is systematic rather than noisy. For the same reason we attach no interpretation to the flatness of the accommodative-Fed profile in \autoref{fig:headline}, which tilts in either direction under modest changes in the cut: what is robust is the \textit{contrast} between the two states, not the shape of the response within either. We therefore read the evidence as establishing that euro area long-rate pass-through is weaker when the Fed is restrictive, without asserting a point estimate for how much weaker.
