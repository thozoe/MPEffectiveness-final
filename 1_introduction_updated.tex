%First paragraph
In response to the post-Covid-19 inflation surge, major central banks tightened monetary policy, but the timing and pace of tightening differed substantially across countries. Whereas the Federal Reserve (Fed) and the Bank of England raised rates relatively early, the European Central Bank (ECB) responded more gradually. Such divergence may generate important cross-border policy interactions, particularly because monetary policy co-moves internationally amid strong financial and real linkages. Yet most policy models specify central-bank reaction functions exclusively in terms of domestic conditions \citep[see, e.g.,][]{del2013frbny}, largely abstracting from foreign monetary-policy spillovers beyond their indirect effects through trade and domestic macroeconomic variables. This paper addresses this omission by modelling the ECB's policy stance as explicitly dependent on the Fed's policy stance, given the Fed's central role in the global financial cycle \citep{miranda2020us}.

In this paper, we estimate a Bayesian smooth transition vector autoregression (ST-VAR) for weekly euro-area financial data whose dynamic coefficients depend, through a logistic transition function, on the Fed's policy stance. We measure this stance as the deviation of the effective federal funds rate from the US natural rate of interest \citep{laubach2003measuring}, which is the rate consistent with output at potential and stable inflation. That is, positive (negative) values signal a restrictive (expansive) Fed. The transmission of ECB policy surprises to euro-area yields and market-based inflation expectations is thereby allowed to vary smoothly with the Fed's stance.

For this, we need a model in which the transmission mechanism itself is allowed to move. Large-scale multi-country models capture international linkages well and are the natural tool for counterfactual and conditional forecasting exercises \citep[among many others,][]{dees2007exploring, feldkircher2016international, georgiadis2017bi, koop2016model, burriel2018uncovering, dees2021global}. In a linear model, however, the propagation of a given shock is invariant to the foreign policy environment by construction: a conditional forecast changes the path of the shocks, not the dynamic coefficients that map a shock into outcomes. Since our question is precisely whether those coefficients depend on the Fed's stance, we need them to be an explicit function of it. Three further design choices follow from the object of interest. We work at weekly frequency, which preserves the speed of financial transmission while limiting the noise of daily data. We identify ECB surprises from high-frequency market reactions in a narrow window around Governing Council announcements \citep{altavilla2019measuring}, purged of information effects following \citep{jarocinski2020deconstructing}. And we compress fifteen maturities of nominal yields and inflation-linked swaps into Nelson-Siegel factors \citep{DIEBOLD2006337}, which lets us trace the response of the entire term structure (nominal, real, and inflation compensation) rather than that of a single benchmark rate.

Our central finding is that the Fed's stance does not shift the pass-through of an ECB surprise up or down: it \textit{rotates} it. The model places the Fed in restrictive territory from the start of the sample until the onset of the financial crisis, again for about eighteen months in 2018--19, and then continuously from mid-2022 to the end of the sample. When the Fed is accommodative, a contractionary ECB surprise raises nominal euro area yields by a similar amount all along the curve, about 1.3 basis points at one year and one and 1.5 at thirty. When the Fed is restrictive, the same surprise raises the one-year yield by more but the reaction then falls monotonically with maturity and is no longer distinguishable from zero at thirty years. The state dependence is therefore absent at the short end and grows with maturity.%, reaching about one basis point at thirty years with a posterior probability of $0.97$ of being positive. 
Decomposing the nominal response shows where this reflects a change in composition and where it does not. At the long end it does not: the real response fades along with the nominal one. At the short end it does and substantially so: inflation compensation falls about three times more when the Fed is restrictive, so that the one-year real-rate response exceeds its nominal counterpart by more than half. An ECB tightening delivered while the Fed is tight therefore tightens real short-term financing conditions appreciably more than its nominal footprint suggests. The sign and the maturity profile of this pattern survive changes in how the Fed's stance is measured, in how the ECB surprise is identified, and in how the regime boundary is calibrated; the magnitude is less well determined, varying by a factor of about two and a half across those choices, and we report it as a range rather than as a point estimate.

\textbf{Literature. } Our paper connects to three strands of work. The first documents international monetary policy spillovers, mostly running from the US to the rest of the world and back \citep{kim2001international, canova2005transmission, bluwstein2018beggar, crespo2019spillovers, ca2020monetary, breitenlechner2021spillbacks, ca2021making, gj2023global}. \cite{ca2021making} find sizeable effects of US shocks on euro area macroeconomic and financial indicators, and considerably weaker effects in the opposite direction, with the largest responses on financial markets. The second studies the global financial cycle and the dominant role of the dollar. These supply the channels through which a foreign stance could plausibly matter: a loose Fed compresses risk premia worldwide and encourages leverage \citep{miranda2020us}, and highly rated euro area bonds are close substitutes for Treasuries, so that the pricing of euro area duration depends on the Fed-driven return on the dominant safe asset \citep{gopinath2021dominant}. We do not attempt to adjudicate between these channels and therefore treat them as interpretations consistent with the maturity profile we document rather than as mechanisms we test. The third strand studies state dependence in monetary transmission over the \textit{domestic} business cycle, with mixed results \citep[see, e.g.,][]{tenreyro2016pushing}. Our conditioning variable is the foreign policy stance, not domestic slack. While existing work establishes that foreign policy shocks spill over and that domestic transmission varies with domestic conditions,we analyze the effect of a \textit{domestic} shock conditional on the \textit{foreign} policy stance.
