\sug{We have asked whether the transmission of ECB policy to euro area financial markets depends on the stance of the Federal Reserve, using a smooth transition VAR in which the dynamic coefficients vary continuously with the deviation of the federal funds rate from the US natural rate. The Fed's stance does not scale ECB transmission up or down; it \textit{rotates} it. When the Fed is accommodative, a contractionary ECB surprise raises euro area yields by a similar amount along the entire curve. When the Fed is restrictive, the short end responds somewhat more strongly while the long end ceases to respond at all. State dependence in ECB transmission is therefore a phenomenon of the long end, and it takes the form of a pivot of the pass-through curve around its short end rather than a uniform weakening of transmission.}

\sug{Decomposing the nominal response into real rates and inflation compensation locates where the Fed's stance changes the composition of the adjustment and where it does not. At the long end it does not: the real response fades along with the nominal one. At the short end it does. Inflation compensation falls about three times more when the Fed is restrictive, so that the one-year real-rate response exceeds its nominal counterpart by more than half. An ECB tightening delivered while the Fed is tight therefore tightens real short-term financing conditions by considerably more than its nominal footprint suggests, and it does so by lowering expected inflation rather than by raising the nominal rate. Because the model is specified in first differences, the rapid mean reversion of the weekly responses is consistent with persistent effects on the level of yields: under an accommodative Fed a surprise leaves a level shift of about one and a half basis points that has not unwound after half a year, whereas under a restrictive Fed no lasting level effect remains.}

\sug{We have been deliberate about what this evidence does and does not establish. The sign and the maturity profile of the state dependence survive replacing the stance measure with a shadow short rate, replacing our composite surprise with information-purged shocks identified by a different rotation, and moving the calibrated neutral stance over a range that reclassifies up to fourteen per cent of the sample. Its magnitude does not: the long-horizon gap varies by a factor of about two and a half across these choices, most of that traceable to how the surprise is identified rather than to where the regime boundary is placed, and we accordingly report it as a range rather than as a point estimate. Nor do we separate the channels that could generate the pattern, which would require decomposing yields into expected short rates and term premia. Two extensions follow naturally. The logistic form of the interaction could be relaxed, letting the data determine how transmission depends on the Fed's stance --- for instance through a time-varying parameter VAR whose coefficients are nonparametric functions of the stance, modeled with regression trees \citep{hauzenberger2025bayesian}. And the system could be extended beyond euro area quantities to a genuinely bi-country setting, which we leave for future research.}
