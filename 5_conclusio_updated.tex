\section{Concluding remarks}\label{sec:conclusio}
\rev{In a globalized world, monetary policy depends not only on prevailing macroeconomic fundamentals but also on how central banks interact, because key transmission channels have an explicit international dimension and effective policy hinges on international spillovers in financial and real markets as much as on domestic first-round effects. In this paper, we develop an empirical weekly time series model that captures, in a nonlinear fashion, the interactions between domestic macro-financial dynamics in the euro area (EA) and the US monetary policy stance. We identify shocks using readily available high-frequency instruments designed to capture unexpected movements in monetary policy, and we measure the US policy stance from an estimate of the neutral interest rate.}

\rev{In sum, our results reveal a systematic interaction between US and EA monetary policy. Rather than relying on a comparison of two extreme stances, we characterize the transmission of an ECB surprise across the entire range of the Fed's stance. For nominal bond yields this mapping is clean and monotone: a contractionary EA surprise raises euro area yields significantly when the Fed is accommodative, and this reaction weakens steadily as the Fed turns restrictive, and vanishing at the long end under a strongly hawkish Fed.} \rev{The short end tells a different story from the long end. A contractionary surprise raises one-year yields somewhat \textit{more} when the Fed is restrictive than when it is accommodative, so the state dependence we document is not a uniform weakening of transmission but a pivot of the pass-through curve around its short end. Because the model is specified in first differences, the mean reversion of these weekly responses is consistent with persistent effects on the level of yields: under an accommodative Fed a surprise leaves a level shift of about one and a half basis points that does not unwind over a half-year horizon, whereas under a restrictive Fed no lasting level effect remains.}

\rev{Our model also lets us examine real interest rate reactions by taking the difference between the nominal-yield and inflation-swap responses draw by draw. This decomposition shows that the muted nominal reaction under a restrictive Fed conceals a change in composition rather than an absence of transmission. Inflation compensation falls when the Fed is hawkish at every maturity out to fifteen years, and markedly so at the short end; under an accommodative Fed it falls too, but by about a third as much, and the difference between the two regimes is itself significant only at one and three years. The one-year real rate consequently rises by more than half again as much as the nominal rate when the Fed is restrictive. An ECB tightening delivered into a hawkish Fed environment thus tightens real short-term financing conditions more forcefully than its nominal footprint suggests, and does so by lowering expected inflation rather than by raising the nominal rate.}

\rev{We have been deliberate about what this evidence does and does not establish. The rotation itself is not an artifact of our measurement or modeling choices: it survives replacing the funds-rate gap with a shadow short rate as the stance measure, replacing our composite surprise with information-purged shocks identified by a different rotation, and moving the calibrated boundary between the regimes over a range that reassigns a fifth of the sample. Across all of these the sign and the maturity profile of the state dependence are preserved. Its magnitude is not: the estimated long-horizon difference between the accommodative and restrictive responses varies by a factor of about two and a half across specifications, most of that traceable to how the surprise is identified rather than to where the regime boundary is placed. We therefore conclude that euro area long-rate pass-through is weaker when the Fed is restrictive, without putting a point estimate on how much weaker. The post-pandemic tightening cycle of 2022--2024, during which the model identifies a pronounced and sustained restrictive Fed regime, is what supplies the identifying variation at that end of the stance.}

\rev{The model interacts foreign monetary policy with domestic quantities in a simple and transparent way. A natural next step is to relax the logistic form of the interaction and let the data determine how the transmission depends on the Fed's stance --- for instance through a time-varying parameter VAR whose coefficients are nonparametric functions of the stance, modeled with regression trees \citep{hauzenberger2025bayesian}. Such an approach would accommodate richer, potentially non-monotone patterns of state dependence than the two-regime mixture imposed here.} In addition, we have only included EA-based quantities and capture international spillovers mainly by including the US monetary policy stance. Estimating larger bi-country models would in principle be feasible, which we leave for further research.
